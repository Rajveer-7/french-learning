Pourquoi ça marche comme çaPourquoi le français compte par vingtaines

Quatre-vingt-dix-sept looks absurd until you see it as arithmetic: (4×20) + 17.

French inherited a base-20 counting system — probably from Celtic, which counted in twenties — layered on top of the Latin base-10. Standard French kept the twenties only from 70 upwards; Belgian and Swiss French kept the tidier septante, huitante, nonante.

So there is no word for seventy, eighty or ninety at all. Know soixante, quatre-vingt and 0–19, and you already know every number to 99.

1 · Numbers review — 69 to 100, plus corrections

Regional note: France says soixante-dix for 70. Belgium/Switzerland instead say septante ("sep-tont") — good to recognise, but use soixante-dix for standard France French / TEF Canada.
Corrections from the whiteboard: un = "a/one" (masc., "an") · une = "a/one" (fem., "yune") · et ("and") = "ay" · 11 is onze, not "dix et un" (a common mistake!) · 71 is soixante et onze — the "et" is still needed even past 69.

3 · Jeu de couleurs — Colour game

Nommez les couleurs que vous voyez. — Name the colours you see. Prompts used: des macarons, au restaurant, au marché.

New structure — il y a = "there is / there are". Used to point out what's in a picture: il y a du blanc, du rouge... (there's some white, some red...).
